# What I Learned About Customer Discovery

Customer discovery sounds simple in theory: talk to your users.

But doing it well is one of the hardest things I had to learn.

When I first started talking to potential users, my instinct was to pitch. I wanted to tell them about the product I was building, show them the features, and ask them if they would use it.

I quickly learned that this is the fastest way to get bad data.

## People Want to Be Nice

If you ask someone, "Would you use this feature?", they will almost always say yes. They don't want to hurt your feelings. But a "yes" to a hypothetical question is not evidence of a problem worth solving.

True customer discovery isn't about validating your idea. It's about validating the problem.

## Asking Better Questions

I learned to stop asking about the future and start asking about the past.

Instead of asking: "Would you use a tool that helps you discover trusted vendors?"
I now ask: "Tell me about the last time a trusted vendor cancelled on you last minute. How did you find a replacement?"

When you ask people to describe past behavior, they tell you the truth. They tell you about the desperate phone calls they had to make. They tell you about the risk they had to take on an unverified stranger. They tell you about the actual friction they experienced.

## Discovery Is an Investigation

Customer discovery is not a sales pitch. It is an investigation.

My role is to be a systems researcher. I need to understand why the problem exists, who experiences it, and what workarounds they are currently using to survive it. If they aren't actively trying to solve the problem today, they probably won't pay for my software tomorrow.

The most valuable skill I developed wasn't writing better code. It was asking better questions.
