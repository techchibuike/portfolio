# What Isolation Taught Me

When I first decided to take my education into my own hands, I spent years in what most people would call isolation. Outside of lectures and church activities, I spent nearly all my time learning, building, researching, and thinking.

There was no structured mentorship. No expensive bootcamps. Just curiosity, consistency, and discipline.

At the time, I believed that sacrifice was completely necessary. And looking back, I still do. 

## The Gift of Independence

That season of isolation gave me something that formal education often fails to teach: the ability to learn independently. It forced me to develop deep focus, patience, and persistence. When I didn't know how to solve a problem, there was no one to ask. I had to figure it out. 

That built a foundation of technical resilience. I learned that I could adapt quickly to whatever challenge was in front of me, simply because I had trained myself to learn difficult concepts on my own.

## The Cost of Isolation

But that isolation came with a high cost.

Because I was so focused on building my technical competence, I completely neglected networking. I rarely attended events. I didn't build professional relationships. I fundamentally underestimated the value of visibility.

I thought that being competent was enough. I thought the work would speak for itself.

I was wrong. 

## The Shift

Eventually, reality taught me a hard lesson:
**Being competent is valuable. Being visible multiplies that value.**

Ideas grow faster when they are connected to people. Opportunities don't just fall out of the sky; they travel through relationships. 

This realization changed everything for me. I no longer view networking as "meeting people" or "schmoozing." I view it as creating opportunities for collaboration, learning, and impact. 

I am learning to balance solitude with collaboration. Isolation built my foundation, but connection is what will allow me to build something that actually scales.
