# Curiosity Needs Evidence

Curiosity is a powerful trait for a founder, but it can also be a trap. 

If your curiosity is untethered from reality, it simply becomes a distraction. You end up chasing interesting technical challenges or exploring hypothetical markets without ever grounding those ideas in facts.

## The Principle

**Curiosity asks the question, but evidence must provide the answer.**

## The Danger of Hypotheticals

It is incredibly easy to sit in a room with a whiteboard and imagine a problem. *“What if organizers struggle to trust vendors? What if we built a verification system?”* 

Those are good questions. They are driven by curiosity. But if you stop there—if you assume your hypothetical scenario is true and begin building a solution based on it—you are building on a foundation of sand.

Curiosity will give you a hypothesis. But a hypothesis is useless without data.

## Seeking Evidence

When curiosity leads you to a new idea, the immediate next step is not to build it. The next step is to test it.

- How can I prove myself wrong?
- What must be true for this idea to work?
- What evidence exists right now that contradicts my assumption?

You must seek out real-world evidence. Talk to customers, run small experiments, and observe actual behavior. 

If the evidence supports your curiosity, proceed. If the evidence contradicts your curiosity, change your mind. The market rewards understanding, not imagination.
